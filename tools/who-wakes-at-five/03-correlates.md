# 03 — Correlates of Early vs. Late Chronotype, Early Waking, and Waking With Difficulty

**Scope:** lifestyle, demographic, occupational and health correlates of early vs. late chronotype and early wake times; the experience of waking early *with difficulty*; and the "5am club" claim.
**Geography:** United States vs. rest of world (UK, Finland, Czechia, Japan, EU and multinational samples).
**Literature cutoff:** searched 2026-10-19. Includes 2026 releases (BLS ATUS 2025, released June 2026; BLS Job Flexibilities 2024–25, released Sept 2026; Kianersi et al. JAHA 2026).
**Method:** I verified each number against primary text (abstract or full text via Europe PMC, BLS tables, the Census Reporter API for ACS 2024, and the UK Biobank Showcase). Values I computed are marked **[derived]**. Inferences beyond the data are marked **[Speculative]**.

**Corrections to the brief:**
- The 2025 snooze study is **Robbins et al. 2025, *Scientific Reports*** (Brigham & Women's/Harvard with SleepCycle AB), not "Ruder et al." Its headline figure is **55.6%** of sleep sessions ending with snooze.
- The 2024–25 UK Biobank chronotype–CVD paper is **Kianersi et al., *JAHA* 2026** (preprint 2025), not Makarem.
- In Daghlas 2021, the "23% lower risk" is the estimate per **1-hour-earlier sleep midpoint**. It is genetically proxied, not a wake-time intervention.
- Roenneberg 2012's "69% ≥1 h / one-third ≥2 h" social-jetlag figures: the publisher blocked the primary PDF, so I could not read them there. See §5.1 for what I could verify.

## (a) Key findings

### 1. Who actually wakes at 5 a.m. in the US? Mostly workers on early shifts, and mostly men in production, transport, protective services and healthcare
- **About 16% of Americans aged 15+ are not asleep at 5:00 a.m., and about 34% at 6:00 a.m.** In ATUS 2025, 84.3% were sleeping at 5 a.m. and 65.9% at 6 a.m. (all days; BLS Table A-3, 2025) **[derived: 100 − % sleeping]**. These shares were essentially unchanged from the pre-pandemic 2015–19 average (84.3% and 65.0% sleeping).
- **14.3% of US commuters leave home before 6:00 a.m., and 9.4% before 5:30** (ACS 2024 1-year, B08302; universe: 143.3M workers 16+ who did not work from home) **[derived]**.
  - **Men: 18.4%. Women: 9.5%** (B08011).
  - **69% of pre-6 a.m. departers are men** **[derived]**.
- **Early departers have longer commutes.** People leaving 5:00–5:29 a.m. average **35.1 min** one way, against **27.2 min** for all commuters (B08133 ÷ B08302) **[derived]**.
- **State range for leaving before 6 a.m.:** Arizona 19.7%, Hawaii 19.2%, Louisiana 18.9%, Nevada 18.5%, Alabama 18.1% at the top; New York 10.1%, Vermont 10.5%, New Jersey 10.9% and DC 5.8% at the bottom (ACS 2024) **[derived]**.
- **By occupation, share at work at 5:00 a.m.** (ATUS 2021–25, BLS Table A-4; employed people on days worked):
  - Transportation and material moving 20.2%
  - Protective service 16.4%
  - Production 15.1%
  - Healthcare support 11.9%
  - Healthcare practitioners 11.4%
  - Farming, fishing and forestry 10.3%
  - Compare: all workers 6.2%; computer/math 1.2%; legal 1.2%; arts/media 1.2%.
- **At 6:00 a.m.:** production 35.4%, transport 32.2%, construction 20.5%, building/grounds 19.4%, healthcare support 18.5%; all workers 13.6%.
- **Construction is an early-start, not off-shift, occupation.** It is 96.2% "regular daytime" (BLS 2024–25), yet 52.6% of construction workers are on the job by 7 a.m.
- **Early work start costs sleep.** Basner et al. 2014 (ATUS 2003–11, N=124,517): people starting work at or before 6 a.m. slept about **6.0 h**, against **7.29 h** for 9–10 a.m. starters. Each hour later that work or class starts adds about **20 min** of sleep.
- **Income is not a strong driver of early rising.** No rigorous US paper found. One low-quality survey (US-Mattress, n≈500, 2016) found no income gradient in waking before 7 a.m.
- **What this means for the "5am club" image** **[Speculative]**: in the US, the population awake at 5 a.m. is dominated by shift and early-start blue-collar, protective and healthcare workers with longer commutes. It is not dominated by self-selected early-rising executives. Treat any "5am riser" persona as a mostly occupational group.

### 2. Exercise and physical activity: evening types are less active (consistent, modest, observational)
- **Systematic review:** Sempere-Rubio et al. 2022 (23 studies, N=505,375) found evening chronotypes associated with less physical activity and more sedentary time. The association is weaker in young and university-age samples.
- **UK Biobank (Kianersi et al. 2026, JAHA, N=322,777):** share with a poor Life's Essential 8 (LE8) physical-activity score (<50 points):
  - Definite morning: **22.2%**
  - Intermediate: **25.1%**
  - Definite evening: **31.9%**
  - **[derived from published counts]**. Adjusted prevalence ratio for evening vs. intermediate: 1.26 (1.24–1.29); for morning: 0.88 (0.86–0.89).
  - Note: under the AHA LE8 algorithm, a PA score <50 roughly means fewer than 60 min/week of moderate-to-vigorous activity. That is my reading of the AHA scoring, not a figure stated in the paper.
- **Accelerometer data, Northern Finland Birth Cohort 1966 at age 46 (Nauha et al. 2020, N=5,156, 14-day wrist accelerometry):**
  - Morning-type men did **+98.6 MET-min/day** (95% CI 30.2–167.1) more total activity than evening-type men, and **−38.6 min/day** sedentary time (−56.9 to −20.2).
  - Morning-type women did **+57.8 MET-min/day** (10.5–105.0). There was no difference in women's sedentary time.
- **Finland, FINRISK (Wennman et al. 2015, N=4,904):** "evening" and "tired more-evening" types had higher odds of no or very low leisure-time activity than "morning" types.
- **US baseline:** **22.5%** of US adults met both the aerobic and muscle-strengthening guidelines in 2022 (NHIS; CDC MMWR 2024). I found **no US study reporting the % meeting guidelines by chronotype**.
- **"Of those who get up at 5am, how many have consistent exercise routines?"** I found no study that answers this directly (see Gaps). The closest proxy is ATUS time-of-day data:
  - 0.5% of Americans 15+ are doing sports or exercise at 5 a.m., 1.3% at 6 a.m., and 1.8% at 8 a.m. (2025).
  - So of those awake at 5 a.m., about **3%** are exercising at that moment, and about **4%** at 6 a.m. **[derived, point-in-time, not routine]**.
- **Causality:** no robust Mendelian randomization (MR) evidence that chronotype causes activity levels. One MR in UK Biobank (Huang et al. 2026) points the other way: more genetically predicted sedentary time worsens sleep traits. Treat the chronotype → physical activity link as **correlational, possibly bidirectional**.

### 3. Family structure and household
- **Parents wake earlier.** In a YouGov survey (Sept 2019, n=1,272 US adults), **56%** of parents of children under 18 wake by 6 a.m., against **37%** of non-parents. Parents also snooze more: **65%** hit snooze at least once, against 49% of non-parents. This is an opt-in online panel, so treat it as indicative only.
- **Children shift mothers' timing.** Leonhard & Randler 2009 (Germany, n=179 women): having children predicted greater morningness and roughly **1.5 h earlier weekend rise times**. Mothers were more synchronized with their child (r=0.56) than with their partner (r=0.40).
- **Social jetlag by family situation** (Kudrnáčová & Hamplová 2022, Czech Household Panel 2018, N=1,441 workers):
  - Parents of children aged 0–5 had about **10 min less** social jetlag than childless workers. This makes sense: children enforce early weekend wake-ups.
  - There was no effect for older children.
  - Living with a partner was linked to lower social jetlag only before work variables were added.
  - Self-employed workers averaged 0.87 h of social jetlag, against 1.27 h for employees.
- **Time pressure, US (ATUS 2025):** employed adults with a child under 6 averaged **3.2 h/day** of leisure, against **4.5 h** for employed adults without children under 18. At 6 a.m., 0.9% of the population is caring for household children, rising to 2.6% at 7 a.m. (BLS A-3, 2025).
- **Marital status:**
  - I found no large, well-validated estimate of wake time by marital status.
  - ATUS analyses show married mothers *sleep less* than never-married or divorced mothers net of work hours (Pepin, Sayer & Casper 2018, *Demography*, ATUS 2003–12). That paper reports duration, not timing.
  - Assortative mating by chronotype is not supported in short-term couples (Witek et al. 2025, n=100 couples). Partners' wake times are nonetheless correlated.
- **National wake times by presence and age of children (ATUS microdata):** these exist in principle but no peer-reviewed table was found. I could not compute them because bls.gov blocks bulk downloads from this environment (see Gaps).

### 4. Shift and early work in the workforce (US vs. EU)
- **US, 2024–25** (BLS Job Flexibilities and Work Schedules, ATUS Leave module, released 2026-09-24): wage and salary workers by usual shift:
  - Regular daytime: **85%**
  - Evening: **5%**
  - Night: **3%**
  - Rotating, irregular, split or other: about **7%**
- **Non-daytime work by industry:** leisure and hospitality **40%**, transportation and utilities **25%**.
- **Why people work non-daytime schedules:** nature of the job 36.6%, personal preference 23.6%, better family arrangements 12.5% (18.0% for women, 8.6% for men).
- **BLS does not publish an "early-morning shift" category.** Early starts are folded into "regular daytime." Use the ATUS A-4 hour-of-day table (§1) and ACS departure times as the early-start measures.
- **EU-27, 2025** (Eurostat LFS): **17.4%** of employees work shifts; **4.1%** of employed people usually work nights and **7.4%** sometimes do. The US and EU definitions differ, so these are not directly comparable.

### 5. Health correlates (forest-plot table in §b.1)
- **Mortality: small association, largely explained by lifestyle.**
  - Knutson & von Schantz 2018 (UK Biobank, N=433,268, 6.5 y, 10,534 deaths): definite evening vs. definite morning **HR 1.10 (1.02–1.18)** for all-cause mortality.
  - Hublin & Kaprio 2023 (Finnish Twin Cohort, N=23,854, 37 y, 8,728 deaths): evening vs. morning **HR 1.09 (1.01–1.18)**. The authors attribute the attenuation mainly to smoking and alcohol. There was **no excess mortality among non-smokers who drank lightly at most**, and no increase in any cause-specific mortality. They conclude "little or no independent contribution of chronotype to mortality."
  - No newer 2024–25 mortality reanalysis was found beyond Hublin 2023 and the Kianersi CVD mediation result. A 2026 NHS II preprint (Kianersi et al., medRxiv) on shift work × chronotype and mortality is not yet peer-reviewed.
- **Cardiovascular disease: about 75% mediated by modifiable risk factors** (Kianersi et al. 2026, JAHA; UK Biobank, N=322,777; 13.8 y; 17,584 events).
  - Definite evening vs. intermediate: **HR 1.16 (1.10–1.22)** for CVD.
  - After adjusting for all LE8 components: 1.06 (1.00–1.12).
  - LE8 explained **75%** of the association.
  - Definite morning types were **not** at higher risk: 1.03 (0.998–1.07).
- **Type 2 diabetes** (Kianersi et al. 2023, *Annals of Internal Medicine*; NHS II, US nurses, N=63,676; 1,925 cases):
  - Definite evening vs. definite morning: **HR 1.72 (1.50–1.98)** after adjusting for sociodemographics, shift work and family history.
  - After adjusting for all lifestyle factors: **HR 1.19 (1.03–1.37)**.
  - Evening types were 54% more likely to have an unhealthy lifestyle.
  - **MR (Jones et al. 2019, N=697,828): no causal effect of chronotype on BMI, fasting insulin or T2D.**
- **Metabolic markers** (Zhang et al. 2022 meta-analysis, 27 studies, non-shift workers), evening vs. morning:
  - BMI **+0.44 kg/m²** (0.30–0.57)
  - Fasting glucose **+5.83 mg/dL** (3.27–8.38)
  - HDL **−1.80 mg/dL** (−2.30 to −1.31)
- **Prevalent disease** (Knutson 2018, definite evening vs. definite morning): psychological disorders **OR 1.94 (1.86–2.02)**, diabetes 1.30 (1.24–1.36), neurological 1.25, gastrointestinal 1.23, respiratory 1.22.
- **Depression: the strongest causal signal.**
  - Daghlas et al. 2021 (JAMA Psychiatry; two-sample MR; exposure GWAS N=697,828; MDD 170,756 cases / 329,443 controls): genetically proxied morning preference scaled to a **1-h-earlier sleep midpoint** gives **OR 0.77 (0.63–0.94)** for MDD. Results were similar for stringently defined MDD (0.73, 0.54–1.00) and non-significant for hospital-coded MDD (0.64, 0.39–1.06). The Egger intercept was null.
  - Jones 2019 MR: morningness → lower MDD (OR 0.65, 0.44–0.95), lower schizophrenia (0.89, 0.82–0.96) and higher well-being (+0.04 SD).
  - Observational meta-analysis (Norbury 2021, 43 effects, N=27,996): eveningness–depression correlation is small, **Fisher's z ≈ −0.24 (−0.27 to −0.21)** as morningness increases. The abstract prints "−2.4", which appears to be a typo given the CI. Heterogeneity was high and there is possible publication bias.
- **Breast cancer:** two-sample MR (Richmond et al. 2019, BMJ; BCAC 122,977 cases / 105,974 controls): morning preference **OR 0.88 (0.82–0.93)** per category increase.
- **Cognition: evening types score slightly higher.**
  - West et al. 2024 (BMJ Public Health; UK Biobank, N=26,820, aged 53–86): evening vs. morning **β = +0.135 SD (0.090–0.180)** in Cohort 1 (4 tests) and **+0.075 (0.032–0.118)** in Cohort 2. Intermediate types were also higher than morning types.
  - Older meta-analysis (Preckel et al. 2011): eveningness–cognitive ability **r = 0.08**; morningness–cognitive ability **r = −0.04**.
  - All cross-sectional; very small effects.
- **Academic performance: morning types do better** (Preckel 2011 meta). Morningness–achievement **r = 0.16** (N=3,220); eveningness–achievement **r = −0.14** (N=700). Part of this runs through conscientiousness and school start times.
- **Personality** (Lipnevich et al. 2017 meta, 44 samples, N=16,647):
  - **Conscientiousness–morningness ρ = .37**, the strongest link.
  - Unique β: conscientiousness .32 with morningness; extraversion .23 and openness .17 with eveningness; neuroticism −.16 with morningness.
  - Big Five traits explain 10.9–16.4% of variance in chronotype. Buzoku et al. 2025 replicated the conscientiousness link.
  - Snoozers are lower in conscientiousness and more evening-oriented (Mattingly 2022).
- **Substance use:**
  - Alcohol (Sirtoli 2023 meta, 13 studies): evening types **OR 1.41 (1.16–1.66)** for consuming alcohol.
  - Substance use disorder diagnosis (Steardo 2025 meta, 5 clinical studies): **OR 1.55 (1.20–1.95)**.
  - Smoking (UK Biobank, Kianersi 2026): poor nicotine score (<50) in **10.1% / 11.4% / 20.0%** of morning / intermediate / evening types **[derived]**; adjusted PR 1.62 (1.58–1.66).
  - Caffeine (Ágoston 2019, Hungary, N=2,259): morningness goes with tea; eveningness with cola, energy drinks and caffeine use disorder. No pooled estimate.
- **Diet timing and eating behavior:**
  - UK Biobank poor diet score: **28.3% / 33.6% / 38.8%** (morning / intermediate / evening) **[derived]**.
  - Later chronotype correlates with binge eating **r = 0.39**, night eating 0.21 and food addiction 0.17 (Arab et al. 2026 meta, 51 studies).
- **Socioeconomic status and income: mixed direction.**
  - In UK Biobank, definite evening types more often have a degree (**43.3% vs. 33.6%** of definite morning types) but higher deprivation (Townsend −1.0 vs. −1.4).
  - Finland, NFBC1966 (Conlin et al. 2023, N=5,020): evening chronotype has an indirect wage penalty of about **−4% for men**, running through less work experience and poorer health.
  - Finland, genetic study (Hazak et al. 2025, N=20,121, polygenic instruments): **morningness genes are associated with *lower* education** but also lower odds of the lowest income quintile in men. Highly educated "genetic owls" get lower income returns.
  - Denmark (Bonke 2012): morning types earned significantly more, especially men. No effect size was available to me.
- **Mendelian randomization scorecard:**
  - Supports a causal role for morningness: **depression** (Daghlas; Jones, suggestive), **schizophrenia**, **well-being**, **breast cancer** (Richmond).
  - No causal effect: **BMI, T2D, fasting insulin** (Jones 2019).
  - Associations largely explained by confounding or mediation: **mortality** (smoking and alcohol; Hublin 2023), **CVD** (75% mediated by LE8), **diabetes** (lifestyle explains most of it).
  - Education and income: genetic evidence is bidirectional and context-dependent (Hazak 2025).
  - No MR found for physical activity, smoking or alcohol as outcomes of chronotype.

### 6. Waking with difficulty
- **Alarm clocks:** **"over 80%"** of the MCTQ population use an alarm clock on workdays (Roenneberg 2023, *Nature Reviews Endocrinology*; MCTQ database). This is the primary-author statement behind the "~80%" figure.
- **Snooze** (Robbins et al. 2025, *Scientific Reports*; SleepCycle app; 21,222 users; >3M sleep sessions; 43.6% of users in the US):
  - **55.6%** of logged sleep sessions ended with snooze.
  - When snoozing, users pressed it 2.4 times and snoozed **10.8 min** on average.
  - **45.2%** of classified users were "heavy" snoozers (>80% of sessions).
  - Snooze use is higher on weekdays and higher among women.
  - By country: Sweden 2.7 presses and 11.7 min; **US 2.5 presses and 11.3 min**; Japan lowest at 2.2 presses and 9.2 min.
- **Snooze, US worker sample** (Mattingly et al. 2022, *Sleep*, n=450): **57%** snoozed. Snoozing was more likely among women, younger people, those with fewer steps, lower conscientiousness, more disturbed sleep and **later chronotype**.
- **Self-reported difficulty getting up** (UK Biobank, N=497,285, aged 40–69, 2006–10; Showcase field 1170): **18.0%** find getting up "not at all easy" (4.0%) or "not very easy" (14.0%); 49.5% "fairly easy"; 32.2% "very easy".
- **Morning tiredness as a chronotype marker** (Finland, FINRISK, Wennman 2015): latent classes were "rested more-evening" 28%, "rested more-morning" 24%, "morning" 23%, **"tired more-evening" 17%** and "evening" 8%. Morning alertness was the key feature separating the types.
- **Sleep inertia and "sleep drunkenness"** (Ohayon et al. 2014, *Neurology*; US representative sample, N=19,136): **15.2% (14.6–15.8%)** had confusional arousals in the past year. Only 0.9% met criteria for confusional arousal disorder.
- **Social jetlag** (difference in mid-sleep between free days and workdays):
  - Rutters et al. 2014 states that "in recent Internet-based studies, **69%** of all participants reported at least 1 h social jetlag" (citing Roenneberg's MCTQ work). In its own Dutch sample (n=145): 37% ≤1 h, 37% about 1 h, **26% ≥2 h**.
  - Wikipedia and Kudrnáčová cite "**>70%** have ≥1 h" from Roenneberg 2012.
  - Czechia (Kudrnáčová & Hamplová 2022, N=1,441 workers): **54%** ≥1 h and **15%** ≥2 h; mean 1.2 h.
  - Japan (MCTQ, N=3,708): **40.1%** ≥1 h and **11.6%** ≥2 h. In people in their 20s, 61.0% ≥1 h and 24.9% ≥2 h; in their 60s, 14.5% ≥1 h.
  - US: NHANES 2011–14 actigraphy (Freeman et al. 2026, *Sleep Health*, N=13,656) shows social jetlag peaking at ages 10–30 and highest among non-Hispanic Black adults. Prevalence cut-points were not accessible to me (no open full text).
- **Insufficient sleep (US):**
  - **35.5%** of adults sleep <7 h (BRFSS 2022, via America's Health Rankings). This is the "about one-third" CDC figure.
  - By state (CDC, 2022): **30% (Vermont) to 46% (Hawaii)**. Men 37%; ages 45–64, 39%.
  - Hawaii and Arizona combine high early departure (§1) with high short sleep. That pairing is an observation, not a tested link.

### 7. The "5am club" claim vs. the evidence
- **No study shows that waking at 5 a.m. per se causes productivity or success.** The evidence is:
  1. Morningness correlates with conscientiousness (ρ=.37) and grades (r=.16). These are trait correlations, and conscientiousness plausibly drives both.
  2. Morningness correlates weakly with proactivity (r=.11; Randler 2009, n=367 students).
  3. Evening types score *slightly higher* on cognitive tests (West 2024; Preckel 2011, r=0.08).
  4. Genetic morningness predicts *lower* educational attainment in Finland (Hazak 2025).
  5. The only randomized evidence for shifting *earlier* (Facer-Childs et al. 2019, *Sleep Medicine*, n=22 night owls, 3 weeks) found about a **2-h advance with no loss of sleep duration**. It improved depression and stress scores, morning reaction time and grip strength. The intervention fixed sleep and wake times, used morning light and set meal times; it did not prescribe a 5 a.m. alarm. It is small and short-term.
  6. Waking early at the cost of sleep has measurable costs. Starting work at or before 6 a.m. goes with about 6.0 h of sleep against 7.3 h (Basner 2014). Quasi-experimental ATUS evidence (Gibson & Shrader 2018, *REStat*) finds a 1-h increase in average weekly sleep raises earnings **1.1% in the short run and 5% in the long run**.
- **Bottom line** **[Speculative synthesis]**:
  - Early rising that preserves sleep and fits one's chronotype is benign or modestly positive. The depression MR supports earlier *sleep timing*, not a fixed early alarm.
  - Early rising imposed on evening types produces social jetlag, short sleep and snoozing.
  - The success correlation is mostly selection (conscientious people and morning types self-select), plus occupational sorting: most actual 5 a.m. risers are early-shift workers.

### b.9 "Waking at 5 a.m. with difficulty" — triangulated estimate [Speculative; no direct measurement exists]
- **Base:** about **15.7%** of US people 15+ are not sleeping at 5 a.m. (ATUS 2025).
- **Assumption range for difficulty among them:**
  - Lower bound: the UK Biobank general rate of finding getting up not easy (**18%**).
  - Upper bound: the share of app-tracked sleep sessions ending in snooze (**55.6%**).
- **Implied range:** roughly **3%–9% of the US 15+ population** wake by 5 a.m. with difficulty.
- **Why the true share is probably above the lower bound** **[Speculative]**:
  - Early risers are disproportionately men in their working years on fixed early schedules, which raises forced-early waking among non-morning types.
  - On the other hand, the UK Biobank figure is from older adults (40–69), who are earlier chronotypes.
- **Treat as an illustrative band, not a statistic.**

## (c) Gaps and caveats
1. **No dataset links wake time ≥5 a.m. to exercise *routine*, family structure and difficulty waking at once.** Every "of 5am risers, X% …" figure must be triangulated. ATUS has no chronotype item and no difficulty-waking item. UK Biobank and NHANES have chronotype and accelerometry but no work start times comparable to ATUS.
2. **ATUS microdata: wake time by presence and age of children, marital status, income or education.** These could be computed: wake time is the end of the first sleep episode after the 4 a.m. diary start. I could not do it here because bls.gov blocks bulk downloads (HTTP 403) and the CRAN `atus` archive only has aggregated activity durations without clock times. **Recommended next step:** IPUMS-ATUS extract (free account) to produce a "wake before 5:30/6:00" table by occupation, children's age, marital status, income and education.
3. **ACS departure time by occupation and income** requires PUMS (JWDP × OCCP). The Census API now requires a key, so this was not computed. The published AASHTO brief (ACS 2011) gives sex and race only: 27% of men vs 15% of women left before 6:30 a.m.
4. **Roenneberg 2012 "69% ≥1 h; about one-third ≥2 h":** the primary PDF was inaccessible (403 from Cell and the Groningen repository). The 69% figure is confirmed as quoted in Rutters et al. 2014; "one-third ≥2 h" is not confirmed. A newer primary-author statement (Roenneberg 2023) gives ">80% use alarm clocks" but no SJL cut-points.
5. **US chronotype × % meeting physical activity guidelines:** not found. UK Biobank LE8 physical activity scores are the best large-scale proxy.
6. **Marital status × chronotype or wake time:** no large validated estimate found.
7. **Mortality 2024–25 reanalyses:** I found Hublin & Kaprio 2023 and the Kianersi 2026 CVD mediation. A 2026 NHS II preprint on rotating night shift × chronotype × CVD and mortality (Kianersi et al., medRxiv, 2026-09) is not yet peer-reviewed and was not extracted.
8. **Wake-time difficulty by chronotype:** a UK Biobank cross-tab of field 1170 × 1180 would require individual-level data. The Showcase gives marginals only.
9. **Self-selection and representativeness:**
   - SleepCycle users and YouGov panels are not representative.
   - UK Biobank is healthier and older than the general population ("healthy volunteer" bias).
   - NHS II is mostly white women nurses.
10. **Definitions differ:** chronotype measures vary (single-item self-report, MEQ, MCTQ mid-sleep, genetic proxies). "Early waker" in ATUS means not sleeping at a clock time; in ACS it means departure time; in surveys it means self-reported wake time.
11. **NHANES actigraphy (Freeman 2026):** wake-time and social-jetlag values by age, income and race exist but the full text was not open. This is worth retrieving for US-specific wake-time-by-demographic plots.
