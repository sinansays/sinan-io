# 04 — Can people shift their chronotype, and how do they feel about it?

Research date: 2026-10-19. Scope: intervention studies, natural experiments, longitudinal stability, self-report and polls, limits, and subjective outcomes.
Verification: unless a row is marked *secondary*, I checked every number against the primary abstract or full text (PMC or Europe PMC full-text XML, publisher PDF or author manuscript). For each number the tables give the population, N, year and source, plus a quality grade:
- **A**: RCT, meta-analysis or guideline
- **B**: controlled or within-subject experiment, or a large cohort
- **C**: small or uncontrolled study, or survey
- **D**: poll or secondary citation

## (a) Key findings

1. **Late types can be moved earlier by about 1–2.5 h of circadian phase (DLMO) within days to weeks.** Morning light, a fixed earlier wake time and afternoon or early-evening melatonin produce the largest shifts. Representative results:
   - **Facer-Childs 2019 (night owls, 3 weeks):** DLMO advanced about 2 h, sleep/wake times advanced about 1.7–1.9 h, and morning sleepiness, depression and stress scores fell.
   - **Lab protocols:** about 1 h/day for 3 days, totalling 1.7–2.6 h (Revell 2006; Crowley & Eastman 2015).
   - **Camping in natural light:** about 2 h in summer (Wright 2013), about 2.6 h in winter, and about 1.4 h over one weekend (Stothard 2017).
2. **Clinical DSWPD trials show smaller and less consistent effects than lab protocols.**
   - Meta-analysis of melatonin: DLMO advanced 1.18 h and sleep onset 0.67 h (van Geijlswijk 2010; 5 adult trials with N=91, plus 4 child trials with N=226).
   - Best RCT (Sletten 2018, N=116): sleep onset 34 min earlier than placebo, and clinician-rated improvement 52.8% vs 24.0%. However, the post-treatment DLMO did not differ significantly from placebo (0.73 vs 0.24 h).
   - The AASM 2015 guideline gives timed melatonin for DSWPD only a **WEAK FOR** rating. For adults it makes no recommendation on light monotherapy, prescribed sleep scheduling or timed exercise, citing insufficient evidence.
   - Light-therapy meta-analyses show small-to-medium effects: Hedges' g = 0.41 for circadian rhythm sleep disorders (van Maanen 2016), and ES = −0.34 for advancing delayed sleep timing (Faulkner 2019).
3. **The shift reverts quickly once the intervention stops.** Being a late type seems to need continuous counter-pressure rather than a one-off fix.
   - ADHD+DSPS adults: DLMO was back at baseline 2 weeks after melatonin±light ended (van Andel 2021).
   - Youth with DSPD: only those who continued light+melatonin for 3 months kept the advance; stopping caused relapse (Saxvig 2014).
   - DSPS clinic patients (Dagan 1998): 91.5% reported relapse within 1 year of stopping melatonin, and 28.8% within 1 week.
   - A single "normal" weekend at home delays DLMO by about 1 h (Stothard 2017: +0.98 h). In adolescents, a typical late weekend delays it by 45 ± 31 min (Crowley & Carskadon 2010).
   - Counter-example: CBT plus bright light in adolescents kept its gains at 6 months, although only N=15 were followed up (Gradisar 2011).
4. **Shifting the schedule is not the same as shifting the clock.**
   - Melatonin can advance DLMO without moving sleep times (van Andel 2022).
   - Melatonin can also improve sleep onset without a measurable DLMO shift (Sletten 2018).
   - Early-schedule protocols often cut sleep by about 1 h. Saxvig 2014 found this, and the AASM review notes that most participants in Sharkey 2011 lost total sleep time.
   - Facer-Childs 2019 is the notable exception: sleep duration was unchanged.
5. **Natural experiments show the reverse: when social constraints loosen, people drift later and sleep more.**
   - COVID lockdowns: workday mid-sleep 50 min later and social jetlag about 30 min lower (Korman 2020, N=7,517). In Argentina, chronotype (MSFsc) was 36 min later (Leone 2020, N=1,021).
   - Students during stay-at-home: weekday sleep timing about 50 min later and time in bed about 30 min longer (Wright 2020, N=139).
   - Seattle's 55-min later school start: wake time 44 min later and sleep 34 min longer, with no significant shift in sleep onset (Dunster 2018).
   - DST: activity timing does not adjust to the spring change, especially in late chronotypes (Kantermann 2007).
   - Earlier schedules imposed by society (school, early shifts) mainly cut owls' sleep. They do not convert owls into larks (Juda 2013; Vetter 2015).
6. **Over years, chronotype is moderately-to-highly stable, and most change is a slow drift earlier with age.**
   - NESDA: r = 0.53 over 7 years, with an average advance of only 10.8 min (Druiven 2020, N=1,417).
   - UK Biobank: autoregressive β = 0.82 over a mean of 4.3 years (Burns 2021, n of about 13.6–18k).
   - Estonian Biobank: test-retest r = 0.66 for MSF (Lenneis 2021, N=681).
   - Self-rated **evening types are the least stable category**. Only 44% stayed "evening" over 6 years (Koskenvuo 2007, n=190), and only 34% over 23 years (Broms 2014, n=567 men), compared with 65–68% of morning types.
   - Category reversal is rare: in a 7-year Finnish cohort, only 4 baseline evening types (of 112) were morning types at follow-up (Maukonen 2019, via Druiven 2020).
7. **No survey directly measures "% of self-identified owls who successfully became larks" or how satisfied they are.** No YouGov, Sleep Foundation or academic dataset reports this. The best proxies are:
   - the share of evening types who change category over time (above);
   - relapse rates after treatment;
   - satisfaction measures inside trials. In the only qualitative treatment study (Wilhelmsen-Langeland 2025, n=11 youths with DSWPD), the mean "worth the effort" rating was 72.5/100 (range 60–100). Participants valued alignment with friends and school, but described real costs: lost evening time, time spent at the lamp, and disappointment when the rhythm "slid back".
8. **Why owls only partly shift:**
   - Chronotype is about 50% heritable in twins (Koskenvuo 2007) and SNP h² is 13.7% (Jones 2019). People carrying the most morningness alleles sleep 25 min earlier than those carrying the fewest.
   - DSWPD patients have longer intrinsic circadian periods (tau) (Micic 2021).
   - Rare variants such as CRY1Δ11 (allele frequency up to 0.6%) lengthen the circadian period (Patke 2017).
   - The light-dark cycle has to keep pushing every day. Later chronotypes gained more from camping (Wright 2013: r = 0.75), which suggests their lateness is partly environmental and therefore reversible only while the environment changes.

Clinical guideline summary: **AASM 2015** (Auger et al., *JCSM*), adult DSWPD:
- Timed melatonin: WEAK FOR. Evidence quality LOW; the supporting trials disagreed.
- Light monotherapy, prescribed sleep scheduling alone, timed exercise, evening light avoidance and hypnotics: **no recommendation** (insufficient evidence).
- Children and adolescents with DSWPD: melatonin WEAK FOR; light therapy with or without behavioural treatment WEAK FOR.
- The draft's meta-analytic figures for adults on 5 mg melatonin: PSG total sleep time +41.4 min (depressed subgroup, n=28); sleep-onset latency −43.5 min (n=28) and −37.7 min (non-depressed, n=12).

Light-therapy meta-analyses:
- van Maanen 2016 (*Sleep Med Rev*; 53 studies, N=1,154): g = 0.41 for circadian rhythm sleep disorders, 0.39 overall; smaller effects in RCTs; evidence of publication bias.
- Faulkner 2019 (*Sleep Med Rev*; 40 studies, 49 comparisons): advance of delayed sleep timing ES = −0.34 (p = .010); sleep continuity ES = −0.23.

**Typical achievable advance (plot as a range bar):**
- Healthy late types with strong protocols: 1.5–2.6 h.
- DSWPD clinical populations: 0.5–1.2 h (DLMO); sleep onset 0.5–0.7 h earlier.
- Exercise alone (3 days): about 0.7–0.9 h.
- Meals: none on the master clock.

Qualitative themes from Wilhelmsen-Langeland 2025 (Norwegian youths aged 16–25 with DSWPD, after 3 months of light and melatonin):
- **Benefits:** falling asleep more easily, more alertness, being able to attend school, sharing a rhythm with roommates, better self-view.

## (c) Gaps and caveats

1. **No direct measure of "successful self-transformation".** I found no academic survey or poll (YouGov, Sleep Foundation/NSF, CivicScience, Ipsos) asking self-identified owls whether they have tried to become, or have become, morning people, or how they feel about it. The proxies used here:
   - category stability: 34–44% of evening types stay evening over 6–23 years, but full reversal is rare (≤ about 4%);
   - relapse rates (91.5% within 1 year);
   - in-trial satisfaction (72.5/100; 96.7% "helpful").
   Category change in cohorts mixes real change, ageing, regression to the mean and measurement error. Moving from "definitely evening" to "somewhat evening" counts as a change even though the person is still an owl.
2. **The Koskenvuo 2007 and Broms 2014 transition numbers are secondary** (taken from Druiven 2020). I could not open the full transition matrices; the publisher pages were paywalled or failed to load. The destination of movers is unknown, so a full 3×3 Sankey cannot be built honestly.
3. **UK Biobank** has repeat chronotype answers (~20k at the 2012–13 repeat, ~100k at imaging), but no published category cross-tab was found.
4. **Persistence is rarely measured with DLMO** after intensive protocols in non-clinical owls. Facer-Childs 2019, Wright 2013, Revell 2006 and Crowley 2015 had no follow-up after the intervention.
5. **Most intervention studies are small** (N = 5–50; only Sletten 2018 exceeds 100) and short (days to 4 weeks). Adherence is often self-reported.
6. **Exercise and meal timing** have no RCTs in self-identified owls with chronotype as the outcome. The exercise PRC comes from a lab ultrashort-cycle protocol; meal timing shifted peripheral rhythms only.
7. **AASM 2015**: I could not open the final published full text (paywalled), so I verified the meta-analytic numbers against the AASM public-comment draft. The final wording of the recommendations matches the published abstract. I did not find an AASM update to the DSWPD guideline as of October 2026.
8. **Parenthood**: only cross-sectional evidence was found (Leonhard & Randler 2009). There is no longitudinal before/after-birth study of chronotype.
9. **The Wescott 2026 secondary outcomes** (satisfaction, adherence, alertness) come from a news summary. The primary outcomes were checked against the abstract.
10. **The magnitude of seasonal shift** in MSFsc was not extracted as a single number (Allebrandt 2014 and Kantermann 2007 are qualitative in their abstracts).
